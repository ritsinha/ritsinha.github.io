> *I wandered lonely as a cloud*
> *That floats on high o'er vales and hills,*
> *When all at once I saw a crowd,*
> *A host, of golden daffodils.*
>
> — William Wordsworth, 1807

In grade 5 or 6, I had to record a recitation of this poem. I remember the exercise clearly, standing in front of a tape recorder and getting the metre right. The daffodil was an English flower, a Wordsworth flower, something from a different world entirely. I did not connect it to anything I already knew.

[image: blog/images/nargis-narcissus-poeticus.jpg | *Narcissus poeticus* (Recurvus). Photo: Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 3.0.]

I grew up hearing the word *Nargis*. In Urdu and Bengali poetry it is a stock image for eyes, specifically the kind of eyes that are large, heavy-lidded, and a little absent, the eyes of someone looking inward. It appears in ghazals constantly. I had never thought to look up what the flower actually was.

A few weeks ago I came across an image showing the answer: Nargis is the Daffodil. The scientific name is *Narcissus poeticus*, "of poets." The same flower.

That's where it gets interesting. *Narcissus poeticus* is named after the Greek youth Narcissus, who fell in love with his own reflection in a pool and wasted away there. The flower is said to have grown where he died. From that myth came *narcissism* as a concept, which Freud borrowed in 1914 to name pathological self-absorption. From the flower came *nargis* in Persian, which spread into Urdu, Hindi, and Bengali as a poetic convention for eyes.

So the same source — one Greek myth about a boy and his reflection — became:

- a personality disorder in clinical psychology
- a spring flower in English gardens
- a classical poetic image for eyes in South Asian literature
- a perfume ingredient (the flower's fragrance is used in high-end perfumes and Ayurvedic oils)
- a Latin epithet meaning "of poets"

I find that a strange and satisfying kind of convergence. A story meant as a warning about vanity ended up in a psychiatry textbook, a garden catalogue, a ghazal, and a bottle of perfume, none of them obviously connected until you look at the name.

The word *nargis* in Urdu and the word *narcissist* in English are the same word. I had used both for years without knowing that.
